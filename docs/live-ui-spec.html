AlphaMolt · Design specification · August 2026

The live account console

A private control desk where a handful of AI trading strategies share one real brokerage account. This document describes what it is for, what it must express, and what it looks like — before an outside design review, and after the rebuild that review produced.

SURFACE /account → “Live account”  ·  CODE web/components/account/*, web/lib/live-*.ts  ·  STATUS shipped, in use, real money  ·  REV 2 — reviewed, rebuilt 19 Aug 2026

The brief for the reader

This is not a request to tidy up spacing. The live console is the one surface in AlphaMolt where a mistake costs real money, and it has been rebuilt four times in as many weeks — each time to fix a genuine comprehension failure, each time arriving somewhere more honest but not obviously more elegant. It now says everything true. It may say it in too many words, in too many boxes, at too flat a hierarchy.

What we asked for: a point of view on the information architecture and interaction model, not a re-skin. Sections 1–5 give the world the interface has to be true to. Section 6 shows the build that was reviewed and the build that replaced it.

Revision 2 — what happened next

The review came back and was acted on. The console now has a single execution timeline instead of three places that said the same thing, an allocation set in percentages instead of dollar targets that had to sum, one sticky command bar instead of a confirm inside whichever card was last touched, dense aligned rows instead of stacked cards, and a palette where green means a gain and nothing else. Section 10 records what changed and section 11 what is still open.

01

Background

What AlphaMolt is, and where “live” sits inside it.

The product

AlphaMolt is an arena for AI investing agents. A user writes a brief in plain English, hires a team of agents — a screener feeds candidates, buyers evaluate them name by name with an LLM, a reviewer sells — and the swarm trades a paper portfolio seeded with $1M of simulated cash. Portfolios compete on a public leaderboard against each other and against SPY. A user may run up to five paper books, each with its own brief, screen and team.

Everything above is a game with a scoreboard. The live account is the moment it stops being one.

What “going live” means

A live portfolio is a private, real-money follower of one paper book. It has no brief, no team and no opinions of its own. It simply holds the same names in the same proportions as the paper book it copies, sized to its real balance at the broker (Alpaca today; the code runs against a broker-neutral seam so others can follow).

The mechanism is target-weight replication, not trade-by-trade replay: each run computes target_shares = paper_weight × equity ÷ price, diffs against what the follower actually holds, and places orders only for names that have drifted more than 1%. Sells go first to free buying power. Because every run re-derives the target from scratch, a partial fill, a price move or an entirely missed run never accumulates into error — the next run simply re-converges.

Sleeves: several strategies, one account

A retail brokerage gives an individual one account. Running two live strategies therefore means splitting one account, and that is the central fact the interface exists to express. A sleeve is a live portfolio that owns a share of a shared account:

  • Shares are attributed, not held. Of the broker's 15 NVDA, 10 belong to Sleeve A and 5 to Sleeve B. The broker knows nothing about this. Only AlphaMolt's records do.
  • Cash is an allowance, not a balance. A sleeve's cash figure is the most it may spend, enforced at the trade RPC. The account's real cash sits behind all of them.
  • Money owned by nobody is left alone, deliberately. Dividends, interest, fees and fresh deposits land in the broker's cash and move a figure called unallocated. The owner credits it to a strategy when they choose. Auto-attributing a deposit is precisely the guess that silently misallocates real money.

Two invariants are checked before any run trades:

Σ sleeve holdings[symbol]==broker position for symbol
Σ sleeve allowances<=broker cash  (difference = unallocated)
The safety property

Every mirror run sizes off a sleeve's own equity and diffs against its own recorded positions — never the broker aggregate. Passing the aggregate would make a symbol held only by another sleeve look like a position at target weight zero, and sell it in full, with real money, every single run. Three refusals back this up: a shared account whose records disagree with the broker refuses to trade; the whole-book state sync refuses to run at all on a shared account; and two live portfolios following the same paper book is an error, not a silent drop.

Funding, and why positions travel

Moving money into a sleeve larger than the source's spare cash moves the difference in kind: cash first, then a proportional slice of the source's share records. Nothing trades at move time — the broker sees one pooled account, so a transfer is a bookkeeping entry. The receiving sleeve then holds names that are not its own strategy's picks, and owes a restructure: on its next run it trades them into its own book. That interval — where a strategy holds someone else's stocks and the interface must say so without alarming anyone — is one of the sharper design problems here.

02

Why this interface is hard

Three properties that break the patterns most financial UI leans on.

Nothing happens when you click

The website cannot trade. Orders are placed by a Python job running on GitHub Actions. When the owner asks for a sync, the site POSTs a workflow_dispatch and receives HTTP 204 — no body, no run id, nothing to correlate against. The job starts within a minute or two, may run for several minutes, and reports back only by writing rows to the database. There is no request/response, no progress bar, no websocket. A click is a letter posted, not a button pressed.

“Nothing happened” is usually correct

A mirror run legitimately does nothing on most runs, for reasons that are all fine: the US market is shut; nothing drifted past the threshold; the master execution kill-switch is off; the records disagree with the broker so it refused. Before we journaled those reasons, a quiet run and a broken run looked identical, and the only place the difference existed was an Actions log the owner never reads. Silence is the enemy of trust on a real-money page.

Two different clocks

The swarm rebalances the paper book at 07:00 UTC — six and a half hours before the US market opens. So the live account cannot act on a decision at the moment it is made. The automatic path is a market-hours cron at 14:00 UTC (≈30 min after the open) that trades whatever changed overnight, and a 23:00 UTC reconcile after the close. Everything the owner sees at breakfast is a decision that will be executed in the afternoon.

Consequence for design

Almost every state in this interface is a pending state. The interface's real job is not to show a portfolio; it is to make waiting legible — what was asked for, what will happen, when, and whether anything needs a human.

03

The owner and their jobs

One persona. No collaborators, no roles, no permissions.

The only viewer is the account owner. The surface is private, never indexed, and invisible to every other user — a live portfolio is excluded from the leaderboard, the consensus page and every public read. There is no shared view to design for and no audience to impress; this is a room the owner sits in alone with their own money.

JobHow oftenWhat good looks like
“How much should each strategy run?”Weekly-ishSee the split as a proportion, change it, understand exactly what will move before committing
“Is anything happening, or waiting on me?”Most visitsAnswered in one sentence, without inference, including when the answer is “nothing”
“Do it now.”OccasionalA manual run, with a confirm proportional to the risk, and visible acknowledgement that it was asked for
“Bring another strategy live.”RareCreate the sleeve and fund it in one confirmed step, from a paper book they already run
“Is this thing sound?”Constant, ambientThe numbers reconcile visibly; nothing is hidden; refusals are explained in the owner's language

The last job is the one that governs every decision. This is real money moved by machines while the owner sleeps. The interface's most valuable output is warranted confidence — and where confidence is not warranted, an unambiguous statement of that.

Language rule in force

No system vocabulary reaches the screen. Not sleeve, not mirror, not portfolio_accounts, not drift threshold. A sentence like “$4,180.00 of Turnaround Book's holdings aren't its own picks yet — they get traded into them on its next sync” is the house style: the mechanism, in the owner's words, with the number that matters in it.

05

Constraints a redesign must respect

These are not stylistic preferences; each traces to a real failure mode.

ConstraintWhy
Changing the split never places an orderIt re-attributes records inside one pooled account. Conflating it with trading would make a bookkeeping edit feel like a market action — and vice versa.
Anything that places real orders is two-stepFirst click arms, second commits, and the confirm names the consequence in plain words.
A quiet state must be stated out loudAn empty region reads as “something is stuck”. The panel always renders, including “nothing pending”.
Never assert the market is openHolidays aren't modelled, so the code only ever asserts the negative. On a real-money page a confident wrong statement is worse than none.
Broker cash may be unreadableIf the web environment has no broker keys, unallocated is unknown and crediting is disabled — but splitting still works, because moving money between strategies never needs the broker. The UI must degrade to that, not break.

Refusals surface as owner-language sentences“Our records disagree with the broker; that needs reconciling before it will trade again” — never a stack trace, never silence.
Every control lives in one placeThe live portfolio's own page is deliberately read-only and points here. Real-money management has exactly one home.
Works on a phoneThe owner checks this on a phone far more than at a desk.
06

The console, before and after

Both builds reproduced exactly as they render. Rev 2 is what is live today.

Where it lives

The console is a section of /account, the owner's private dashboard, sitting below their paper portfolio cards. It renders only for users who actually have a live Alpaca-backed portfolio provisioned. It is headed by a status chip — Private · real money — and followed by a risk acknowledgement modal shown once per browser.

One console renders per broker account (an owner can in principle have more than one set of Alpaca credentials; almost nobody does, so the account key is hidden when there is only one). Inside it: the pot, the bar, the status panel, one card per strategy, and two folds of rarely-needed plumbing.

How the data reaches the screen

The console is a server-rendered read plus client-side arithmetic. Nothing here is a live socket.

SourceGivesFailure mode
portfolios (mode='live')The sleeve set, grouped by broker account key—
portfolio_accountsAllowance + funding baseline (“ever funded”)—
portfolio_holdings × securities.priceHoldings value, and off-book value vs the paper book's tickersUnpriced ticker contributes 0
Alpaca /v2/accountReal broker cash → unallocatedNo keys or timeout ⇒ null; crediting disabled, split unaffected
portfolio_cash_ledgerMoney that moved, with a reason—
run_logs (dispatch + run)What we asked for, and what the run did — including why it did nothingRead fails open — costs a line, never the page
agent_tradesReal fills booked to a sleeveRead fails open

All the sentences are produced by one pure function, buildHubState, with no imports — no DB, no fetch, no React. It is the copy contract, pinned case by case by a test suite that evaluates the TypeScript from Python. The panel component only paints what that function returns.

The page re-reads the server every 30 seconds only while something is genuinely in flight, pauses when the tab is hidden, and stops after ten minutes — past that, the scheduled run is the mechanism, not the page.

08

The three interactions

Changing the split

  1. Type a target into a card. With exactly two strategies the other side auto-fills, so the split always balances without arithmetic.
  2. The card goes pending — signed delta, amber pill — and the apply block appears inside that card, stating each move in words.
  3. Apply split arms a confirm listing every move, separating the cash part from the in-kind part: “$1,900.00: Momentum Swarm → Turnaround Book ($420.00 cash + ≈$1,480.00 as positions — Turnaround Book trades them into its own picks on its next sync)”.
  4. Yes — apply executes each move atomically. An in-kind move is a single transaction: guarded holding decrements, destination upserts at weighted-average cost, cash, both P&L baselines and both ledger legs. A racing heartbeat fill rolls the whole thing back rather than corrupting the split.
  5. The web action auto-dispatches a mirror run for the receiving strategy, so the restructure starts without a second decision. The daily cron re-converges it regardless, and the amber warning persists until it is done.

If prices moved between render and apply, the targets are rescaled to the fresh pot rather than refused — a split's proportions are the intent, the exact dollars are not. A drift beyond 2% is still refused with an explanation.

Syncing now

  1. Do it now (on a line) or Sync (in a card's Manage fold) arms a confirm reading “This places real orders on your broker account.”
  2. The site POSTs a workflow dispatch, gets a 204, and writes its own journal row — the only reason the console can say “sync requested 2 min ago”.
  3. The panel shows a cyan pulsing “on its way” line and starts its 30-second re-read.
  4. When the job writes its own row, the line is replaced by what the run actually did — placed 4 orders, market shut, nothing to do, or refused.

Bringing another strategy live

  1. Pick an unfollowed paper book from the dropdown; one follower per book, five live portfolios per owner.
  2. It appears as a card at $0, explicitly labelled “never funded”.
  3. Fund it by editing the split — which is where in-kind funding usually first happens.

A user's first go-live is still operator-driven (a CLI bootstrap and a baseline reseed from the real account). Only additional sleeves are self-serve.

Held throughout

The copy contract is untouched — buildHubState still writes every sentence and its fixture test passes unmodified; the timeline sorts by a new kind tag rather than by matching text. 11

Answered, and still open

The six questions rev 1 posed, and where they landed.

Q1 · The editing model — answered

Proportions won. Shares are set as percentages with steppers; the others absorb the difference pro rata so the allocation is always exactly 100%.

The objection — that percentages hide the magnitude of an in-kind move — is handled by showing the dollars as a consequence rather than an input: each edited row states what it would move, split into cash and positions-that-owe-a-restructure, and the review dialog repeats it leg by leg.

Q2 · Summary vs detail — answered

Rows, not collapsing cards. A strategy always shows worth, share, cash, P&L, the book it copies and a health dot; everything situational is behind one disclosure. Nothing collapses to less than that, because a real-money row that hides its cash or its P&L is a row the owner has to open anyway.

Q3 · Making asynchrony visible — answered, partly

A spine, not a chart. Past / now / next, with the fix on the item that needs it. It sequences what was already there rather than adding a new visual language.

Still open: whether a true time axis — with the 07:00 decision and the 14:00 execution actually spaced apart — would teach the two-clock model better than three labelled rows do.

Q4 · Where the confirm belongs — answered

A sticky bottom bar naming the total that moves, and a native <dialog> for the review — so focus trapping and Escape are the platform's job. One commitment, one place, whether one strategy changed or five.

Q5 · Severity without alarm — unchanged

Still amber → red on the same sentence after 48 hours, now shown once instead of twice. The underlying question stands: escalating colour on identical copy may be the wrong instrument, and the interface arguably ought to change shape when work goes stale rather than just temperature.

Worth a second opinion on its own.

Q6 · The identity of the whole — answered

A control desk. Aligned columns, one spine, one commitment bar, colour that means exactly one thing. The page reads as an instrument now rather than a form with a status panel attached.

What we'd still like an argument about

  • The health dot. One strategy's outstanding work is now a coloured dot on its row plus a sentence in the timeline. That removed the duplication — but a dot is a weak signal for “$4,180 of this is not what you think it is”.
  • Five sleeves. The rows were designed against two and tested against three. At five the timeline may be the thing that gets long, not the table.
  • The sticky bar on a phone. It pins above the fold on a surface that also scrolls a table sideways-free. Whether it reads as “pending” or as furniture is a question only real use answers.
  • Steppers vs dragging. The bar shows the proposal but still isn't draggable. Dragging a segment is the most direct expression of “this much” — and the least precise, on a page where precision is money.
